## Where the Name Comes From

The grid puzzle started in America. An architect named Howard Garns designed it in 1979, and Dell Magazines printed it as "Number Place" — a name that described the format but never stuck. The puzzle crossed to Japan, where the publisher Nikoli began running it in 1984.

Maki Kaji, Nikoli's founder, shortened a clumsy Japanese phrase to make the name. The full line was `sūji wa dokushin ni kagiru` — the digits must remain single. From that he carved `sudoku`: `su` for number, `doku` for single. The name is the rule. Every row, every column, every 3x3 box gets each digit exactly once, and "exactly once" is the whole game.

Nikoli held the sudoku trademark in Japan, so Western publishers kept using Number Place for two decades. Then Wayne Gould, a retired New Zealand judge who had fallen for the puzzle in Tokyo, sold a solver he wrote to The Times of London, which started printing sudoku in late 2004. The name went global in months. Kaji died in August 2021, eulogized by the press as the godfather of sudoku.

## What the Name Means for How You Play

Knowing the origin turns the name into a strategy reminder. "Single number" is not decoration; it is the constraint that does all the work.

The fastest beginner habit in sudoku follows straight from the meaning: look for a digit that has exactly one legal home left in a row, column, or box. The name says every digit must be single — so when a 7 has one empty cell left in its row, that cell gets the 7, no guessing.
