Windoku is an ordinary 9×9 sudoku with four extra 3×3 regions: rows 2 to 4 by columns 2 to 4, rows 2 to 4 by columns 6 to 8, and the same pair again at the bottom. Each window must hold 1 to 9 exactly once, like a box. Nothing else changes - no sums, no arrows, no shading on a properly printed board, which is why people who open a windoku without reading the caption solve it as plain sudoku and then wonder why the grid refuses to finish.

We wrote a window-aware solver and dug six boards twice, once with the classic rule set and once with the windows switched on. The windows were worth **8.0 givens**. Our classic digs bottomed out at 24.8 givens on those solutions; the same six came up at 16.8 under windoku rules, a 32% cut. That lands windoku between thermometers (7.8) and anti-knight (11.0) on the running list of variants we have measured the same way.

## Where the Four Windows Sit

Take the boxes off your mental map first. A window is the same size as a box, but offset one row and one column inward, so it lands on four boxes at once instead of one.

In the standard layout the four windows cover **36 of the 81 cells** and never overlap each other. Every box contributes exactly four window cells, and here is where box thinking starts to fail, because those four cells arrive in three different shapes:

- **Corner boxes** contribute all four cells as a 2×2 block at the corner facing the grid centre - box 1, top left, hands over r2c2, r2c3, r3c2 and r3c3.
- **Edge boxes** split theirs between two windows, two cells each, on opposite sides. Box 2, top middle, gives r2c4 and r3c4 to one window, r2c6 and r3c6 to the other.
- **The centre box** is the odd one: its four corners, r4c4, r4c6, r6c4 and r6c6, each belong to a different window.

Peer counts follow from that geometry. A classic cell has 20 peers; in windoku **45 cells keep their 20** while the 36 window cells gain. Sixteen of them move to 23 and twenty move to 24. The biggest gains go to cells a window reaches without sharing a row, column, or box with them: for the top-left window those are r2c4, r3c4, r4c2, r4c3 and r4c4, five cells you would not have connected to that region by eye.

Real talk: my first half hour with windoku was wasted scanning windows like boxes. A window aligns with nothing. You cannot glance at a threesome and spot a repeat, and each of the four shapes is a cross-shaped intersection of four boxes. What fixed it for me was to treat the window list as a fourth pass: after every placement, walk the four cells of that one window, then stop trying to hold the boxes and windows in the same mental picture.

## What the Windows Actually Buy You

The 8.0 givens is the headline. It hides a detail that decides whether a windoku puzzle is honest.

Every one of our six windoku minimums had **at least two solutions under classic sudoku rules**. Dug to 16 or 17 givens with the windows carrying the load, the boards are not solvable as normal sudoku at all. A windoku handed to you without a rule sheet is not a slightly harder sudoku; it is an unsolvable one, and any plain solver you paste it into will report multiple solutions. The solver is not broken. It is missing four units.

We also measured the practical end of the range: dig until the grid finishes on naked and hidden singles alone, no pairs, no guessing. Classic stopped at 24.7 givens. Windoku dropped to **19.0**, so 5.7 fewer - a smaller saving than the 8.0 headline, because demanding an easy solve hands clues back.

Then we took those six singles-solvable windoku puzzles and ran them again with the window rule deleted. All six stalled, frozen with **55 to 63 empty cells** still on the board. Decoration is not what the windows are.

Where do the extra deductions come from? In a first candidate sweep over the six minimum puzzles, **108 of 1,547 bans were window-only** - 6.98%, ranging from 3.6% to 9.3% per board. During the singles solve of the easier set, **51 of 372 placements could not be justified by any row, column, or box** (29 naked singles, 22 hidden). Roughly one move in seven exists only if you are looking at a window.

That ratio explains how the variant feels. No new logic gets learned. Old logic gets re-run on four extra units, and you keep forgetting to check them.

## Why You Cannot Filter a Normal Sudoku Into a Windoku

Windoku looks like the easiest variant to fake - same grid, four extra regions, no arithmetic - so I expected a fair share of ordinary solutions to be window-legal by luck.

We generated **120,000 random classic solutions** and checked all four windows on each: 480,000 window checks in total.

- A single window held nine distinct digits **1.42% of the time**, about **1 in 70**.
- The mean was **6.90 distinct digits per window**. Seven distinct was the most common result at 47.7%, six at 26.2%, eight at 21.0%, and only 0.09% of windows dropped as low as four distinct digits. Nine distinct is rarer than eight by a factor of fifteen.
- **7 boards out of 120,000 had three legal windows. Zero had all four.**

Four windows at 1.42% each works out to roughly 1 in 25 million boards if the windows behaved independently, and 120,000 samples was never going to find one. The retrofit idea dies the same way it does for jigsaw sudoku: all four have to come up clean simultaneously, and the per-unit rate is already tiny.

Generation cost is the other half. Filling an empty classic grid took a mean of **83.7 search nodes** across 20 boards. The same fill with window constraints took **10,659.9 nodes**, median 10,121, worst case 29,177 - about **127 times** the work, with zero restarts on all 20 boards. Cheap to run in code, hopeless to attempt by trial and error.

## Solving Windoku Without New Techniques

Every classic technique ports over untouched: naked singles, hidden singles, pointing pairs, box-line reduction. The word "box" becomes "unit", and you have thirteen units instead of nine. What changes is scan order.

1. **Open on the corner boxes.** Their window cells sit as 2×2 blocks at the inner corner, so a digit placed at r3c3 kills three window mates and three box mates simultaneously. It is the cheapest intersection on the board.
2. **Treat the centre box as a switchboard.** Its four corners belong to four different windows. Any placement at r4c4, r4c6, r6c4 or r6c6 moves a different region each time, so a stuck board deserves a look there before you start on the edges.
3. **Resist the 45-sum shortcut.** Each window sums to 45, same as any nine-cell unit, and it is tempting. It did not help us once: in the six minimum puzzles the fullest window carried 5 givens while every other window carried 1 to 4, so every window had at least four holes. A sum over four unknowns yields pair constraints, not placements. Keep the trick for hand-made puzzles with dense windows.
4. **Count window cells inside your box scans.** When checking whether a digit fits a box, four of those nine cells also answer to a window. Skipping that check is the most common slip, and it throws away naked singles you had already earned.
