Sudoku looks intimidating at first glance: a 9x9 grid, numbers everywhere, no obvious starting point. But under the surface it is one of the simplest puzzle games ever designed. There is exactly one rule, and every technique you will ever learn is just a smarter way to apply it. This guide walks you through the basics from absolute zero, so by the end you can sit down with an easy puzzle and finish it.

## What Is Sudoku?

Sudoku is a logic puzzle played on a 9x9 grid divided into nine 3x3 boxes. Some cells are already filled in; those are called **givens**. Your job is to fill every empty cell with a digit from 1 to 9. The catch is the one rule:

### The one rule

Every row, every column, and every 3x3 box must contain the digits 1 through 9 exactly once.

That is the whole game. There is no arithmetic, no addition, and no hidden math. If a row already contains a 7, you cannot put another 7 anywhere in that row. The same logic applies to columns and boxes.

### One rule, three constraints

Because the rule touches three directions at once, each empty cell is constrained by:

- the 8 other cells in its **row**
- the 8 other cells in its **column**
- the 8 other cells in its **box**

A well-designed puzzle always has exactly one solution, reachable through pure logic. You never have to guess — though sometimes the reasoning gets subtle.

## How to Read a Sudoku Puzzle

Before any technique makes sense, get comfortable with the vocabulary. Nothing here is complicated, but having the words straight makes the rest of this guide much easier.

### Rows, columns, and boxes

- **Row:** a horizontal line of 9 cells, labeled 1–9 from the top.
- **Column:** a vertical line of 9 cells, labeled 1–9 from the left.
- **Box:** a 3x3 block, of which there are nine. Most people refer to a cell by its row and column, like "row 4, column 6" — the same coordinates the solver on this site uses.

### Givens, candidates, and notes

A **given** is a number printed in the puzzle that you must not change. A **candidate** is a digit that could still go in a cell — the set of digits not ruled out by the rule. **Pencil notes** (or just "notes") are the small numbers players write in cells to track candidates. On this site, toggle pencil mode to jot notes while you work.

## Your First Techniques

You do not need a toolbox of fifty strategies to start. Three simple ones will solve almost any easy puzzle and many medium ones.

### Naked singles

A **naked single** is a cell with only one candidate left. Since no other digit can go there, that digit goes in, full stop. Find them by scanning each row, column, and box, and crossing out every digit that already appears in a peer cell.

### Hidden singles

Sometimes a digit has nowhere to go in a row, column, or box except one specific cell, even though that cell has several candidates. That is a **hidden single** — the digit is hidden among other candidates but is the only legal home. For example, if a box still needs a 4 and only one empty cell in that box can take it, the 4 belongs there regardless of what else fits.

### Scanning rows and columns

The most powerful beginner habit is **scanning**: pick a digit, look at one row/column intersection after another, and ask "in this box, which empty cells can still hold a 3?" Do this across all nine boxes, one digit at a time, and you will surface hidden singles without even writing notes.

## Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Everyone falls into the same few traps. Knowing them saves you time and frustration:

- **Filling digits without checking all three directions.** A number may be legal in a row but already present in its box.
- **Ignoring pencil notes.** Notes are the memory you do not have to keep in your head; use them freely.
- **Forcing a digit too early.** If you are not sure, do not commit — mark candidates and move on.
- **Confusing the box with the grid.** The 3x3 box is a separate constraint, not a suggestion.

## How to Practice

The best way to learn is to play, starting at the easiest level. Head to the [play page](/play/) and pick **Easy**. Play with pencil notes on, and do not worry about the timer at first — speed comes after accuracy.

A good routine for a beginner puzzle:

1. Scan for naked singles across every row, column, and box.
2. Write candidates in any cell that is not immediately obvious.
3. Scan digit by digit for hidden singles.
4. Re-check your work when the grid looks nearly full — one wrong digit early makes the end messy.

## When You Get Stuck

Every player hits a wall. If you have filled in all your notes and still see no next move, the puzzle is probably harder than your current techniques — not your fault. Open the [solver](/solver/), enter your current grid, and ask it to explain the next step. Watching how it reasons teaches you the technique better than reading about it. This is also the fastest way to recover from a single mis-placed digit.

## Playing on Paper

Solving on screen is convenient, but plenty of players prefer pen and paper — no timers, no distractions, just a grid and a pencil. The [printable puzzles page](/printable/) generates clean PDF-style sheets you can print at any difficulty, one per page or several at once. Take one to a coffee shop and work through the easy sets; on paper, the habits you build transfer straight back to the screen.
