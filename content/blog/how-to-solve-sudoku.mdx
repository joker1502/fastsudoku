If you have ever picked up a sudoku puzzle, stared at the grid, and put it down — this is the guide for you. There is exactly one rule, and a plain four-step method that will take you from "I have no idea where to start" to finishing an easy puzzle. No jargon you need to memorize. Just a repeatable process.

## The only rule you need

A sudoku board is a 9x9 grid split into nine 3x3 boxes. Some numbers are already filled in (the **givens**). Your job is to fill every empty cell with a number from 1 to 9. The single rule:

> Every row, every column, and every 3x3 box must contain the numbers 1 through 9 exactly once.

That is the entire game. No math, no adding — only logic. If a row already has a 7, no other 7 can go in that row. Same for columns and boxes. Every technique in sudoku is just a clever way to apply this one rule.

## Step 1 — Scan one number at a time

Beginners hunt for "the next empty cell." Instead, pick one number — say 3 — and ask: *where can a 3 legally go?* Walk every row, column, and box and find every spot a 3 could still fit. If a 3 can only go in one spot in a row, box, or column, put it there. Do this for 1, then 2, then 3, all the way to 9. You will be surprised how many numbers fall into place from scanning alone.

## Step 2 — Fill the "only option" cells

Now look at cells, not numbers. Find any empty cell where, after ruling out the numbers already in its row, column, and box, only **one** number remains. That cell is an easy placement — write the number in. Let each placement ripple: a filled cell constrains its whole row, column, and box, so keep re-scanning after every few placements.

## Step 3 — Hunt the hidden single

When nothing is obviously one-option, look for a number that has **one legal home** in a row, column, or box, even if that cell seems to have several options. This is called a hidden single. Scan a unit and ask: "of the spots this number could still take, is there only one left?" Write it in. Hidden singles are what unlock most of a medium puzzle once easy placements dry up.

## Step 4 — Write notes, then re-scan

If you stall, stop guessing. Mark, in each uncertain cell, the **candidates** — the small numbers that could still go there. With notes on the board, the moves you were missing become visible: pairs, and the numbers with only one home. Re-run steps 1 and 2 with your notes and the board usually starts moving again. If it still will not budge, the puzzle is harder than this method covers on its own.

## What to do when you get stuck

Every solver hits a wall eventually. That is not failure — it is a signal that the puzzle needs a technique you have not seen yet, or a stray mistake. Two honest moves:

1. **Re-check your own placements.** A digit you placed (not a given) that breaks a row, column, or box is the usual culprit when a puzzle suddenly feels impossible.
2. **Ask the ([sudoku solver](/solver/))** — load your grid, run it, and read which technique it used for the next step. Watching the solver's reasoning is the fastest way to learn, and it unsticks you in one move.

## The method in one sentence

Scan one number at a time, fill every cell with only one option, then hunt hidden singles, and write notes before you ever think about guessing. That loop finishes easy puzzles, and with practice, medium ones too.
