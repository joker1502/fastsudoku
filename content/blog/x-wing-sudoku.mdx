The **X-Wing** is the first advanced sudoku technique most players learn, and it is the perfect gateway to everything above it. It sounds exotic, but it is really just "a candidate can only live in two places in a row, two rows line up, and that lets you eliminate the candidate." Once you can spot one, puzzles that used to stop you cold start giving ground.

## Set up the idea: where a digit *can't* go

Every sudoku technique is about elimination. Near the end of a hard puzzle you have placed many numbers, and most cells have several candidates. The X-Wing is a way to see that a candidate has been cornered into a shape — and that the shape lets you strike that candidate from cells outside it.

## The shape: a rectangle of same-candidate cells

An **X-Wing** exists when a candidate appears in exactly **two** positions in one row, in exactly **two** positions in another row, **and** those two rows share the same two columns. Picture it: the four candidate cells sit at the corners of a rectangle.

Because that digit must go in the top row at one corner **and** in the bottom row at one corner, and both rows only allow it in those two columns, the digit *must* occupy two diagonal corners of the rectangle. That forces a key fact: **in each of the two connecting columns, the candidate cannot appear anywhere except the two X-Wing positions.**

## The elimination

The payoff: whenever that candidate appears elsewhere in either of the two connecting columns, you may strike it. Those are the eliminations the X-Wing gives you. You are not placing any digits yet — you are clearing candidates so the board's next move becomes visible.

## A worked example

Suppose, after some solving, the candidate **5** appears in exactly these positions:

- In **row 2**, 5 can go only at **column 4** and **column 9**.
- In **row 7**, 5 can go only at **column 4** and **column 9**.

That is the rectangle: rows 2 and 7, columns 4 and 9. Now, the digit 5 must go in row 2 at either (row 2, col 4) or (row 2, col 9), and in row 7 at either (row 7, col 4) or (row 7, col 9). It has to pick the two diagonal corners.

Because of that, **in column 4, a 5 cannot be anywhere except the two X-Wing cells** — and the same is true in column 9. So anywhere else in column 4 that lists 5 as a candidate can have that 5 struck out. Same in column 9. The rectangle has eliminated a whole set of candidate 5s at once.

## How to actually spot it

You are not scanning for a rectangle directly. You are scanning this way:

1. Pick a cell with few candidates and think about one candidate, say again 5.
2. Look across each row for rows where 5 is limited to exactly two columns.
3. Find two such rows that share those *same* two columns.
4. The rectangle found — eliminate 5 from both shared columns everywhere else.

The habit that finds X-Wings is **counting the two-option rows for a single candidate.** It takes practice, but once the pattern clicks you see it without thinking.

## X-Wing is a gateway

The moment the X-Wing makes sense, you have the mental model for the whole advanced family: **swordfish** (three rows, three columns), **jellyfish** (four rows, four columns), and **coloring**. They are all the same idea — a candidate cornered into a shape, then eliminated from connected lines — in more dimensions. Most players never need beyond X-Wing; the rectangle is the one worth mastering.
