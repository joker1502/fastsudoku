Most sudoku advice is a pile of techniques. That is useful, but techniques without an order are like owning tools without knowing when to use them. The best sudoku strategy is not a bag of tricks — it is a **framework**: a repeatable loop you run on any puzzle, easy or expert, that tells you exactly what to try next and when to stop spinning your wheels.

## The core idea: escalate, never guess

A well-formed sudoku has exactly one solution, reachable by logic — never force a digit you cannot justify. The whole strategy is: work from the cheapest move up to the most expensive one, and only move up a rung when the current rung stops producing. Guesswork is not part of this framework, because guessing is how you introduce errors that cost you a finished puzzle.

## The framework, five rungs

### Rung 1 — Single-digit scan

Pick a digit, any digit, and trace where it can go across the whole grid: row by row, column by column, box by box. Ask "where is this digit still legal?" When a digit has exactly one home in a unit, fill it. Do this for all nine digits first. Most easy and many medium puzzles crack here with barely any note-taking.

### Rung 2 — Naked singles

Now look at individual cells. Any cell down to one candidate is an automatic placement. Fill it, and let that placement ripple — every filled digit reshapes the candidates around it. Naked singles often cascade, so re-run rung 1 after each rush of placements.

### Rung 3 — Pencil notes on uncertain cells

When scanning and naked singles stall, stop guessing and start noting. Write the remaining candidates into every uncertain cell. Notes feel like overhead, but they convert "I think maybe" into "here is the exact set." With notes on the board you stop re-deriving the same information.

### Rung 4 — Pairs and hidden singles, with notes

Now hunt the pattern moves: **naked pairs** (two cells in a unit sharing the same two candidates — strike those digits elsewhere in the unit) and **hidden singles** (a digit with one legal home even among other candidates). Run these while notes are fresh. This rung alone lifts you to most hard puzzles.

### Rung 5 — Advanced patterns, then the solver

If pairs fail, the puzzle needs the harder patterns — [X-Wing](/blog/x-wing-sudoku) and its relatives. These only appear once the board is fairly full, and they rarely appear in isolation. Rather than force a pattern you are not sure you see, this is the honest place to consult the [solver](/solver/): load your grid and read which technique it used next. You will learn the pattern and unstick the puzzle in one move.

## Why the framework works

Three reasons this beats technique-hoarding:

- **It is always running.** You never stare at a dead board because you always know the next rung.
- **It is cheap-first.** You never waste energy on X-Wing hunting when a naked single was sitting there.
- **It eliminates errors.** Because you never guess, you never build a puzzle on a wrong foundation.

The same loop that finishes an easy puzzle in minutes also carries you through an evil grid — you simply stay on the higher rungs longer. Difficulty does not change the framework; it changes how far up the ladder you climb.

## Practice the framework

Frameworks only stick when you run them. Head to the [play page](/play/), pick **Easy**, and consciously walk the five rungs instead of solving on instinct. Do a **Medium** next and feel where it asks for rung 3 and 4. Print clean [practice grids](/printable/) and do the same on paper, where there are no hints to lean on.

When the framework stalls, the [solver](/solver/) is not a crutch — it is the fastest way to see which rung you skipped. Read its reasoning, notice the move, and apply the framework again.

## The bottom line

The best sudoku strategy is the one you can run on autopilot: **scan, place, note, pair, escalate.** It turns sudoku from a memorization exercise into a repeatable process, and it scales from your first easy puzzle to your first finished evil one. Build on the [basics](/blog/sudoku-for-beginners), sharpen with [sudoku tips](/blog/sudoku-tips), and stack the [techniques](/blog/sudoku-techniques) one rung at a time. Then go run the loop and enjoy the grid.
